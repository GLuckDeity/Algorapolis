## 1. The Credential Problem

### 1.1 The Two-Sided Verdict

The political sentiment that degrees no longer buy what they promised is real, mass-scale, and partially true — and the framework must hold both halves simultaneously, because each alone produces bad policy. The true half is quantified: recent-college-graduate underemployment reached **41.8% in 2025 Q3** (highest since 2020; the New York Fed's dashboard reports the rate "edged up to 42 percent," and roughly 44% of graduates aged 22–27 work in jobs not requiring a degree), while recent-grad unemployment ran at 5.6–5.8%, its highest since 2021. The New York Fed's *Current Issues* anatomy (Abel & Deitz) establishes the persistence structure: underemployment falls to roughly 40% by age twenty-seven and to the historical average of about one-third for all degree-holders by mid-career — front-loaded, but structural rather than cyclical, with the *quality* of jobs held by the underemployed deteriorating toward low-wage and part-time work. The program-level distribution is thinner still: Freopp's completion-adjusted ROI analysis of roughly 30,000 bachelor's programs finds **23% with negative ROI** (28% when adjusted for non-completion risk), a median program ROI of $160,000, and only about 6% of programs exceeding $1 million; EducationData finds the average BA is *negative*-ROI (−16.5%) across its first decade, taking roughly twelve years to break even. The false half is equally quantified: the median bachelor's-degree holder earns **$1,754 per week against $930** for a high-school-only worker (BLS, Q1 2025); the Minneapolis Fed's 2025 synthesis puts lifetime earnings near $2.8 million versus $1.6 million, with Social Security Administration data showing median lifetime premia of +$900,000 for men and +$630,000 for women — an aggregate premium near $1.2 million — and Freopp finds 72% of bachelor's programs still generate positive ROI for the typical graduate. The honest verdict is distributional rather than aggregate: the degree is not a broken promise for the median holder, but it *is* a broken promise for a quarter of program-holders concentrated among those least able to absorb the loss — and a credential market whose signal degrades at the tails while its price compounds is in structural dysfunction, not cyclical dip.

### 1.2 The Thin Credential Pool and the Debt Overhang

The scale of that dysfunction is measurable in three instruments. First, the debt overhang: total US student debt stands at **$1.7–1.8 trillion** (EducationData: $1.835 trillion at end-2025, up $56.3 billion in 2025 alone) — roughly 6% of GDP — with the New York Fed's Consumer Credit Panel showing a flow into serious delinquency of 7.83% in 2026 Q2 (down from 12.88% a year earlier only as repayment programs ramped), 10.6% of balances ninety or more days delinquent, and TICAS documenting a **default cliff of more than 5.5 million borrowers holding $140 billion+ in default** as of October 2025, with $92 billion+ newly defaulted during 2025. Second, the credential pool itself: the Burning Glass Institute's Credential Value Index finds that **only about 12% of the roughly 1.1 million credentials** in the US market offer a significant wage gain earners would not otherwise have obtained — the vast majority of the credential universe is priced above its measured materiality, with no sovereign data layer on which a buyer can observe that before committing a decade of earnings. Third, the demand side: "Dismissed by Degrees" (Harvard Business School / Burning Glass, 2017) documents degree inflation — employers up-credentialing jobs previously performed by non-graduates — locking out **more than 6 million American workers** from jobs they are demonstrably qualified to perform. Community-college completion completes the picture: 33.7% six-year completion (a record, fall 2016 cohort) against 61.1% across all sectors means the lowest-cost layer of the pipeline — the technician layer on which every reindustrialization plan routes — loses two-thirds of entrants before completion.

### 1.3 Why the Market Does Not Self-Correct

The framework cannot assume this dysfunction self-corrects, because the one mechanism supposed to correct it — employers dropping degree requirements and hiring on skills — has been tested and found mostly announcement. The Burning Glass Institute's 2026 update finds that "many companies have announced dropping degree requirements, but sustained hiring changes remain elusive for most," with real change confined to occupational niches. The theoretical reason is older: Caplan's signaling analysis (*The Case Against Education*, 2018) argues that a substantial share of the education premium is signaling rather than human capital — credentialism as positional good — implying that some education spending is socially zero-sum, that employers rationally free-ride on degrees as a screening device, and that the equilibrium is stable because no single employer internalizes the cost of the convention it imposes on society's youth. Degree inflation is that equilibrium's visible face: the convention ratchets, the locked-out population grows, and the corrective pressure fails at the practice layer. This is the education-side expression of the Globalist Efficiency Trap: a market that prices signaling over production capability, conceals its own distributional costs, and requires for its correction a sovereign measurement layer it has no incentive to build. The DP-EP-2 and DP-EP-5 specifications below are that layer.

## 2. The Skills Substrate Gap

### 2.1 The Offshoring–Atrophy Chain

The second half of the problem is not the credential market but the *absence of a substrate for it to certify*: the claim that offshoring hollowed out the venues where production skills were learned verifies at the ecosystem level, even though its university-specific version remains an assertion. The quantitative bridge is Boehm, Flaaen, and Pandalai-Nayar (NBER w25824, 2019): offshoring by US multinationals explains **roughly one-third of the manufacturing employment decline**, measured in the Economic Policy Institute's plant census: a net loss of **more than 91,000 manufacturing plants** and about 5 million manufacturing jobs since 1997, with total manufacturing employment falling from 19.6 million (1979) to roughly 12.6 million. The mechanism matters more than the number: production skills are tacit, and tacit knowledge lives in plants, lines, toolrooms, and the master–apprentice proximity they sustain; when the venue disappears, the transmission chain breaks — not the curriculum, the *ecosystem*. A CHIPS-era academic analysis states it directly: decades of outsourced manufacturing, assembly, testing, and packaging "diminished the workforce ecosystem, imposing major limitations on" rebuilding. Industry testimony runs the same direction — Anduril's founder: American universities have "drifted away from teaching practical skills" (Fortune, July 2026); ASME: the US is losing the ability to create wealth from its research investment after decades of offshoring — but no systematic curriculum audit exists in either direction, so the university-specific claim enters this research as a credible hypothesis, not a finding. The verdict the dossier records: the system-level claim is well-evidenced, the rebuilding clock is real, and a governance architecture that monitors GDP but not skill stock would have watched thirty years of this atrophy without a single alarm firing.

### 2.2 The Pipeline Arithmetic

The gap the atrophy left is measured with unusual convergence across independent estimators. Manufacturing: Deloitte and the Manufacturing Institute project **3.8 million new employees needed 2024–2033, of which about 1.9 million could go unfilled** (the 2021 estimate: 2.1 million unfilled by 2030 at ~$1 trillion output risk). Semiconductors: SIA/Oxford Economics project ~115,000 new workers by 2030 with a **67,000-worker technical gap** (of which ~39%, 26,400, is technician jobs), while McKinsey runs the combined engineer/technician gap at **59,000–146,000 by 2029**, with only about 1,000 new technicians joining the field each year and a warning that technician training programs "would need to be up and running in 2024" to meet 2029 demand; Deloitte's global figure is 1M+ additional skilled workers by 2030. The latency numbers make these gaps a clock, not a queue: a semiconductor process engineer takes **4–6 years from undergraduate enrollment to independent fab productivity, with the scarcest roles adding 18–36 months of on-the-job training**; fab construction itself runs on 3–7-year timelines (TSMC Arizona 4nm high-volume manufacturing live in 2025 with 2nm toward 2029; Intel Ohio delayed to ~2030); and the trades workforce is aging at the exit margin — median welder age ~39 (~26% under 30 in the older series), more than half of skilled-trades workers 45+ (McKinsey, 2024). One calibration honesty is required: the headline gap numbers are employer-survey-derived projections, and the standard labor-economics critique notes that persistent vacancies at stagnant real wages indicate wage-and-conditions problems as much as training-pipeline problems — so the pipeline is one lever, credential esteem and pay parity (EDU-16) another, and the simulation treats skills-gap figures as employer-demand forecasts, not ground truth.

### 2.3 The Know-How Clock

The deepest finding of this research area is that money cannot compress the know-how clock. Miller's *Chip War* accounting puts subsidies at **less than 10% of chip investment** — the constraint on reindustrialization is not capital but the tacit-knowledge carriers (people, not fabs); the $265 billion TSMC commitment, Taiwan-parity yields achieved in Arizona by Q4 2024, and the observed 1.5–3x ramp-slippage factor on defense and semiconductor programs all show that even the best-funded programs buy schedule with difficulty and skill stock only at generational speed. Against this, the transcript's "a generation out" is directionally supported but rhetorically padded: the decade-plus rebuild clock is real (4–6 years per process-engineer cohort, 18–36 months OJT, fab timelines to 2029–2030), while a full ~20-year "generation" has no source — and Germany's capacity to reabsorb ~476,000 new apprenticeship contracts per year in a weak 2025 economy shows the *aggregate* rebuild is feasible at decade scale where institutional pipeline capacity exists. The binding constraint is the specialist layer, where US technician flow (~1,000/year into semiconductors) is two orders of magnitude below need. This is the education-side instantiation of the sufficiency grammar's first factor: skill stock is the scarce reserve that neither capital nor stockpiles can substitute for (tacit knowledge cannot be inventoried), and it carries the longest replenishment lead time in the industrial-sovereignty equation — precisely why DP-EP-3 makes latency a mandatory planning variable.

## 3. Proven Models

### 3.1 The German Dual System and the Co-Governance Core

The German dual system is the standing existence proof that a high-wage economy can run youth-to-occupation transition at industrial scale without a credential arms race: **~1.09 million apprentices in training (2019, across 327 federally recognized occupations**, with BIBB/OECD 2025 counting 320+ and total participation at 1.2–1.3 million), 476,000 new contracts signed even in a weak 2025 economy (against 54,000 unfilled places), and **youth unemployment of 6.5% (October 2024) against an EU average of 15.2%** — the EU's lowest. Three architectural features carry the result, none of them "cultural." First, occupational standards are *co-governed* — employers and unions co-own the content of the recognized occupations through statutory bodies, so the standard is a continuously renegotiated contract between demand and supply rather than either side's projection; Germany reorganized 214 occupations (43 of them new) in a single reform cycle, which is the update cadence a versioned-law system must beat. Second, provision is SME-anchored: **85% of German apprentices are trained in firms with fewer than 500 employees** — the carrying structure is the Mittelstand, not flagship firms, a transferability warning for economies with thin SME bases, where an institutional employer-substitute (Fraunhofer-style institutes as training firms, state-industry clusters) must be designed explicitly. Third, the mechanism is measurable as insurance: an IW study attributes to dual VET a reduction of youth unemployment on the order of ~21% — a macroeconomic shock absorber for precisely the population (young, non-college) that Section 5 identifies as the actual radicalization channel. This is EDU-16's "equivalent certification prestige" and "salary parity norms" operationalized: parity via infrastructure — portable standards, employer co-ownership, statutory certification, wage bands — not slogans.

### 3.2 Small-State Variants: Switzerland, Singapore, Korea

Three smaller systems triangulate the pattern and its boundaries. **Switzerland** routes approximately two-thirds of each youth cohort (some series 70%) into VET after compulsory schooling — the OECD's highest voluntary vocational participation, on the same ingredients as Germany's (portable federal standards, employer provision, permeability to tertiary) at higher per-capita density. **Singapore's SkillsFuture** (2015–) adds the lifelong-learning entitlement layer: roughly 65% of school leavers enter postsecondary VET, individual SkillsFuture Credit accounts make mid-career reskilling a standing right rather than a crisis response, and course registries are industry-validated — UNESCO features it as a global case, and its design directly informs DP-EP-4. **Korea's Meister schools** are the controlled experiment in esteem: 21 schools launched in 2010 on an explicit "employment first" model achieved roughly 90% graduate employment for five consecutive years (2013–2017) and still reported 73.1% employment in 2025 — the highest of any Korean school type — yet vocational graduates increasingly choose college over jobs when the earnings and esteem gap persists. The lesson for EDU-16: employment outcomes are achievable by design; *parity of esteem* is not — it requires the wage-parity and classification machinery (procurement rules, levy rules, occupational wage bands) that makes the certified technician's price signal match the graduate's.

### 3.3 Institutional Funding and Firm–University Compacts

Two further patterns complete the proven set. **The Fraunhofer rule** is the gold standard for production-linked institutional funding: of Fraunhofer's €3.6 billion annual budget (2025, +27% versus 2020), **no more than one-third is base institutional funding; two-thirds must be won competitively** — the bulk as industry contracts from ~7,000 industrial customers per year across 72+ institutes and 20,000+ staff. The rule converts *demand verification into a funding condition*: an institute industry will not pay has already failed its market test, and core funding only ever leverages proven relevance — self-validating demand, the institutional analogue of the external grading that distinguishes East Asian success from import-substitution failure (Amsden's reciprocity, encoded in DP-PC-9). **Firm–university compacts** attack latency at its source: TSMC's joint semiconductor programs with top Taiwanese universities (2019) — joint curricula, internships, guaranteed interviews — plus its six-month STIPT talent incubation, its Arizona Technician Apprenticeship Program, and the ASU partnership constitute the tightest known coupling between a firm's process roadmap and the pipeline that feeds it. The US-native ancestor is co-operative education, founded at the University of Cincinnati by Herman Schneider in 1906: UC co-op students earned $94.1 million in 2024–25 (~$11,220 per student per term across 8,300+ students), with Northeastern co-ops averaging $20+/hour. The **P-TECH 9–14 pattern** (high-school diploma plus applied associate degree in six years, employer mentors, the original Brooklyn 2011 cohort reporting 98.5% of Black students graduating within six years) extends the same bridge into secondary school. Together these define what DP-EP-3 calls the *lowest-latency bridges*: forms in which firm, educator, and student are contractually entangled from day one, so the 4–6-year skill clock starts years before it does in sequential architectures.

## 4. Negative Models

### 4.1 The UK Apprenticeship Levy

The UK's apprenticeship levy (2017) is the cleanest demonstration that spending on vocational form without demand-side design produces the opposite of the intended result. Levy starts fell **26% post-implementation** — from 494,900 (2016/17) to 322,500 (2019/20) — with starts among non-levy-paying employers falling by roughly half; the Public Accounts Committee concluded the reforms were "failing to deliver," and the diagnosis is architectural: a hypothecated tax on employers, spendable on "apprenticeships" as redefined upward into management MBAs and executive courses, was predictably captured by older workers' upskilling at incumbent firms rather than youth entry into production occupations. The levy bought *enrollments* without purchasing a single additional youth-to-occupation transition at the margin that mattered; it has since been redesigned as the Growth and Skills Levy in an explicit admission of failure. The pattern is rent-seeking capture (Variant A of the Autarky/Leverage Trap) in education form: subsidy without demand-validation and without exit conditions flows to whoever holds the reclassification pen, and the intended beneficiaries — young entrants, production occupations — are structurally the worst positioned to capture it. DP-EP-2's anti-gaming caps and completion-adjusted outcome measurement are written directly against this case.

### 4.2 US Trade Adjustment Assistance

The US Trade Adjustment Assistance program is the controlled trial for retraining as practiced, and the result is negative: the classic DOL-commissioned evaluation (Decker, Corson, et al., 1995) found TAA training delivered **no earnings advantage over ordinary reemployment services**, and Brookings' standing verdict is that the program "continues to disappoint" — low participation, negligible earnings impacts. The mechanism, established by Jacobson, LaLonde, and Sullivan (AER 1993): high-tenure displaced workers suffer **persistent annual earnings losses of roughly 25%**, replicated at 18–35% in Canada and persisting fifteen-plus years in Germany — mid-career displacement destroys occupation-specific human capital that classroom retraining does not rebuild, because the loss is not of credentials but of tacit, firm- and line-specific knowledge. "Learn to code" fails for the same reason offshoring succeeded: the knowledge that mattered was never in the classroom. What works is documented in the same literature: Denmark's flexicurity (generous income support with *mandatory activation* attached to training, employer co-financed, accepting high churn as the price of transition) and Singapore's SkillsFuture entitlement with industry-validated registries — both tying training to verified employer demand and paying for placement rather than seat-time. The generalizable law the framework encodes: **retraining tied to employer-validated demand and paid placements works; classroom retraining untethered from demand does not; pure income support alone is cheap but does not restore earnings trajectories.**

### 4.3 Manpower Planning: The Autarky Trap in Education Form

The third negative model is not a program but a temptation: pure manpower planning — the state forecasting occupational demand N years out and routing quotas of students into the forecast slots. This is the education-form expression of the autarky trap, and its failure mode is not injustice but *forecast error*: planned-economy occupational quotas fail because demand uncertainty (technology, trade, taste shocks) makes the five-year occupational forecast a fiction, and system inertia converts every forecast error into cohort-scale misallocation — the Soviet occupational-planning apparatus and the import-substitution economies' targeted manpower programs share this signature, and the failure compounds through the latency clock of §2.3 (a mis-forecast cohort occupies a 4–6-year pipeline slot the correct cohort needed). The Algorapolis position is the explicit algorithmic middle path between the two traps: not pure market signaling (which produced degree inflation, a 12% materiality pool, and 6 million locked-out workers — §1), and not quota planning (misallocation at cohort scale), but **versioned occupational standards continuously updated against a measured demand feed** (DP-EP-1), funding that follows verified placements rather than seat-time or quota (DP-EP-2), and latency disclosure (DP-EP-3) so the polity's plans carry their own lead-time error bars. The demand signal becomes governance data; the plan remains a forecast — priced, versioned, and falsifiable.

## 5. The Debt–Radicalization Question: An Honest Verdict

### 5.1 What Is Established

The causal chain from debt to *psychological distress* is solid and multiply-replicated: Lindgren et al. (2022) find student debt associated with anxiety, depression, and problem drinking, with effects strongest among those in lower socioeconomic positions; a 2026 systematic review concludes debt contributes to materially higher financial anxiety as a mental-health concern; surveys agree at scale — 65% of borrowers report debt-driven anxiety or stress (Harvard CLP), 62% report mental-health harm (CNBC), 43.5% report hopelessness and 41.5% depression (2024 borrower survey). The young-cohort *ideological drift* is also measured fact: 62% of Americans aged 18–29 hold a favorable view of socialism (Cato, 2025), Gallup's positive image of capitalism slipped to 54%, and Harvard Youth Poll (51st edition, December 2025) finds 43% of young Americans financially struggling while "capitalist" self-identification fell from 29% to 19% between 2020 and 2025. Indebted youth lean redistributive: 71% of voters 18–34 support canceling student debt for all (SBPC, 2022); 88% of current borrowers support forgiveness (Cato, 2022).

### 5.2 What Is Not

What is *not* established is the composite causal claim that indebted underemployed graduates become furious anti-system radicals — and the direct evidence cuts against the "furious dropout" framing: a 2025 study finds Americans with student loan debt are significantly **more likely to vote and engage electorally** than their counterparts without debt. Debt mobilizes participation; it does not visibly detach citizens from democracy. Meanwhile, the well-identified insecurity-to-extremism channel runs through an entirely different population — **non-college workers in trade-shock regions**: Autor, Dorn, Hanson, and Majlesi ("Importing Political Polarization?", AER 2020) show China-shock commuting zones shifting toward *both* ideological extremes as long-term joblessness and insecurity push communities to the edges; Fetzer ("Did Austerity Cause Brexit?", AER 2019) shows UK welfare-cut exposure driving UKIP support sufficient that, absent it, "the EU referendum could have resulted in a Remain victory"; Colantone and Stanco tie Leave vote share to local import competition; Guriev and Papaioannou's synthesis (JEL 2022) documents populists gaining 10–15 percentage points across Europe on unemployment, inequality, and trade shocks. The radicalization risk concentrates among secondary-educated unemployed and underemployed men — the population the credential inflation of §1 locks out, not the population it enrolls. No study isolates student debt as a cause of extremist affiliation, and the youth-drift attribution is confounded by generational cohort, housing costs, and post-2010 polarization.

### 5.3 The Encoding Verdict

The framework's encoding decision is therefore exact: **debt enters the architecture as a distress-exposure and volatility variable, not as a causal radicalization rule** — raising the volatility of youth political preferences and interacting with underemployment to amplify anti-system voting, with elasticities drawn from the trade-shock literature rather than an assumed debt-to-extremism law. The policy corollary is equally exact: income-contingent, risk-sharing credential finance is justified by the distress evidence and the default cliff alone (5.5 million borrowers, $140 billion in default) — the radicalization argument is not needed to justify it, and a framework that reaches for a dramatic but unproven causal story where an honest one suffices will pay for the rhetorical profit in scientific credibility. This research program states the point as a design norm: **honesty about contested causal claims is what distinguishes a governance science from an ideological tract.**

## 6. Distributional Stress-Test Finding

The distributional finding is the quietest in the evidence base and the most consequential for finance design: **credential-market dysfunction is regressive.** The concentrations align: debt's psychological harms are strongest among borrowers in lower socioeconomic positions (Lindgren et al., 2022); underemployment concentrates among lower-SES graduates; Black borrowers face default disparities documented in Pew's 2024 analysis; and the negative-ROI program tail of §1 is occupied disproportionately by the for-profit and low-completion segment that serves exactly these borrowers — while community colleges, the lowest-cost rung of the pipeline, leak two-thirds of entrants before completion (33.7% six-year completion). The mechanics compound through the debt instrument itself: a lower-SES borrower who draws a negative-ROI credential bears the principal, the interest, the distress externality, and the default risk, while the median borrower's $1.2 million premium is cited as the system's justification — aggregate ROI averages away precisely the population the education substrate research (Part IV, EDU-13 Cognitive Justice, EDU-20 Credential-Wealth Decoupling) identifies as the system's equity obligation. The design rule follows: **any education-finance mechanism must be stress-tested against distributional outcomes — underemployment, default, and debt-distress by SES and by group — not just aggregate ROI.** An instrument that passes the aggregate test while concentrating default cliffs in the bottom quartile is a failing instrument, whatever its mean. DP-EP-6 wires this test into the simulation layer: distributional failure is a stability variable for the civilization, not a charity line item.

## 7. Design Principles — The Education-to-Production Pipeline (DP-EP-1 through DP-EP-6)

**Principle 1 — Occupational Standards as Versioned Law (DP-EP-1):** The SLE shall maintain portable occupational standards co-governed by employers and educators as versioned law — the German dual-system pattern of 327 federally recognized occupations with employer co-ownership and statutory certification, updated on a published cadence that must beat the German benchmark (214 occupations reorganized, 43 new, in one reform cycle). The National Digital Twin shall carry a live feed of vacancy and wage data per occupation, and material divergence between the standard's implied skill set and the measured vacancy/wage signal triggers a mandatory standard-review event. Standards are demand-validated contracts between production and education, never either side's unilateral projection. This operationalizes EDU-16 (Parity of Esteem Architecture) at its production end: portability plus statutory certification is what makes the vocational certificate a currency rather than a consolation. *Evidence base: German dual system (1.09–1.2M apprentices, 327 occupations, 6.5% youth unemployment, 85% SME provision, 476K new contracts 2025); NDT vacancy/wage feeds (6f findings, rule 1).*

**Principle 2 — Funding-Follows-Verified-Placements (DP-EP-2):** A defined portion of program funding shall be tied to completion-adjusted employment outcomes, published as a standing sovereign data layer — Freopp-style program-level ROI and Burning Glass-style credential wage-gain measurement institutionalized as public infrastructure rather than episodic research. Employer co-payment is the demand-verification anchor, with the Fraunhofer ≤1/3 base-funding rule as the gold standard: institutions dependent on competitively won industry contracts carry their own demand test, and a provider whom employers will not co-pay has already failed the market exam. Anti-gaming caps bound the mechanism — caps on redirection (institutions may not improve measured outcomes by shedding hard programs or hard students), completion-adjustment (outcomes credited only for completers, so creaming enrollment does not pay), and floor funding sufficient to prevent the manpower-planning capture of §4.3. This is the algorithmic middle path between pure market signaling and quota planning, written directly against the UK levy (starts −26% while spending rose). *Evidence base: UK apprenticeship levy failure; Fraunhofer two-thirds competitive funding; Credential Value Index (12% of 1.1M credentials with significant wage gain); 6f findings, rule 2.*

**Principle 3 — Pipeline Latency Planning Mandate (DP-EP-3):** Talent-pipeline lead times shall be first-class Monte Carlo variables in all capacity policy: process-engineer cohorts at 4–6 years plus 18–36 months of on-the-job training, technician flows measured against the ~1,000/year current semiconductor flow versus tens of thousands needed, and the 1.9M/67K gap horizons entered as explicit clock constraints. No reindustrialization plan may be scored, published, or adopted without disclosing its pipeline lead-time assumptions, because a policy that ignores 5–10-year talent latency shows phantom GDP gains — the 155mm-ramp and fab-timeline pattern (1.5–3x slippage) replicated at pipeline scale. The SLE shall weight funding toward the lowest-latency bridges documented in §3.3: the P-TECH 9–14 pattern (secondary-plus-associate integration with employer mentors and guaranteed-interview structures) and TSMC-style university–industry compacts, which start the skill clock years earlier than sequential architectures. This principle implements DP-PC-6 (Skills Substrate Mandate) at the education side of the boundary and operationalizes EDU-17 (Automation-Resilient VET) by making latency, not seat-time, the planning primitive. *Evidence base: semiconductor lead-time data (4–6 years + 18–36 months OJT); SIA/McKinsey/Deloitte gap figures; P-TECH and TSMC–university compact records; 6f findings, rule 3.*

**Principle 4 — Retraining Guarantee Bundle (DP-EP-4):** Every displaced-worker guarantee shall bundle three components as a single legal instrument: an income floor during transition (the Jacobson–LaLonde–Sullivan finding of ~25% persistent annual earnings losses defines what the floor must bridge), a placement-contingent training payment (providers paid on verified placement in the re-employed occupation, not on enrollment — the TAA verdict makes anything else a transfer to training providers), and an employer demand pre-commitment (training runs only against employers who have contracted hiring intent for the target occupation, the Singapore SkillsFuture and Denmark flexicurity pattern). The bundle buys outcomes, not enrollments; its twin failure modes are documented and named — the US variant (too little, too late, unlinked) and the pure-manpower variant (misallocation at cohort scale). The guarantee is the education-side complement to the intervention ladder of DP-PC-4: retraining is the cheapest rung of adjustment only when demand-anchored, and an unanchored guarantee is not a rung but a cost. *Evidence base: TAA evaluations (1995; Brookings); JLS 1993 and replications; Denmark flexicurity; Singapore SkillsFuture; 6f findings, rule 4.*

**Principle 5 — Credential Materiality Rule (DP-EP-5):** Public-sector and licensed occupations may not require credentials whose registered wage-gain evidence fails a materiality test — the sovereign data layer of DP-EP-2 supplies the registered evidence, and a credential whose measured completion-adjusted wage gain does not clear the threshold may not be a lawful hiring gate for public employment or licensed practice. The rule is deliberately narrow: only demonstrably low-value credential requirements are banned, leaving the rest of the credential market to price signals and private hiring autonomy — regulation that polices the measured bottom of the pool (the 88% of the 1.1M-credential universe without demonstrated significant wage gain, concentrated in the "Dismissed by Degrees" requirements that lock out 6M+ workers) rather than presuming to price the whole market. The narrowness is required by the evidence: skills-first hiring has proven mostly announcement, not practice (Burning Glass Institute, 2026), so the market's correction mechanism has failed its test, and the state's substitute intervention must be bounded to exactly the demonstrated failure. Public-sector hiring is the lever: the state is the largest credential demander, and its conventions propagate through the licensed occupations it regulates. *Evidence base: Dismissed by Degrees (6M+ workers affected); Burning Glass Credential Value Index (~12% significant wage gain); skills-first hiring gap (2026); 6f findings, rule 5.*

**Principle 6 — Distributional Stress-Test (DP-EP-6):** Every education-finance mechanism — loan structures, guarantee schemes, funding formulas, subsidy instruments — shall be stress-tested in the Civilization Simulation against distributional outcomes, not just aggregate ROI: underemployment, default, and debt-distress concentrations by socioeconomic stratum and by group, with the lower-SES and Black-borrower concentrations of §6 as the calibration case and the 2025–26 default cliff (5.5M borrowers, $140B+) as the adverse scenario. A mechanism that passes the aggregate test while concentrating default and distress in the bottom quartile fails and may not be adopted or renewed. The test is wired to the regime comparison of Simulation V6 (§8.3): distributional failure is scored as a stability cost on the social-fabric metrics, because the regressive concentration of credential-market dysfunction is not a side effect of the education system — it is the transmission channel through which a growth-positive policy degrades the democratic substrate. This principle extends EDU-13 (Cognitive Justice Audit) from curriculum into finance: the audit question — does this mechanism systematically disadvantage a stratum's or tradition's carriers? — now applies to who bears the debt, the default, and the distress. *Evidence base: Lindgren et al. 2022 (lower-position concentration); Pew 2024 Black-borrower disparities; TICAS default cliff; 6f findings, rule 7.*

## 8. NDT and Simulation Implications

### 8.1 Skills-Stock Accounting as a First-Class Civilization Metric

The NDT's education layer extends downward from enrollment and attainment telemetry into **skill-stock accounting**: tacit-knowledge carriers counted as a first-class civilization metric, per occupation and vintage cohort, at the resolution the German system demonstrates is administrable (327 occupations with vacancy/wage feeds). The metric feeds the Civilization Simulation V6's seventeenth metric, **`industrial_capacity` (IC)**, specified as ECI percentile (0.35) + manufacturing value-added (0.25) + sole-source-inverse (0.20) + critical-goods self-sufficiency (0.20): the first two weights are, at bottom, functions of the skill stock, which is why the education layer is not a social-policy annex but the industrial metric's measurement substrate. The DP-PC-6 rule is the binding cross-reference: **no reindustrialization target may be scored "achieved" while its occupational pipeline fill-rate is below 80% of forecast** — a target claimed on capital expenditure alone with an empty technician pipeline is the phantom-achievement pattern the 155mm and fab-slip data exhibit, and the NDT's fill-rate feed is what makes the rule auditable. The credential ROI registry of DP-EP-2/DP-EP-5 lives in the same layer: completion-adjusted wage outcomes per credential, published as sovereign data — the infrastructure whose absence §1.3 showed to be the market's core failure.

### 8.2 Pipeline Latency in the Monte Carlo Engine

Talent-pipeline lead times enter the V6 Monte Carlo engine as first-class variables, not constant parameters: the `reindustrialization_response` shock carries a 3–4-year rebound lag as its floor, with the specialist-layer clock (4–6-year cohort formation + 18–36 months OJT) modeled as a distribution whose right tail — the 1.5–3x ramp-slippage factor documented across defense and semiconductor programs — governs the pessimistic runs. The consequence is DP-EP-3's doctrine: policies that assume talent arrives with the capital show phantom gains the latency-annotated runs expose, and the variance between naive and latency-annotated runs *is itself a policy output* — a quantified overstatement measure for every proposal that crosses the Agora. The education-side shocks couple to the trade-side: a `supply_chain_weaponization` cascade that raises the sole-source-inverse weight simultaneously raises the shadow price of the affected occupations' fill-rates — the simulation discovering skills gaps before the labor market does. The WWII conversion datum (war output pushed from 2% to 40% of GNP on a pre-existing skill base) calibrates the conversion-speed ceiling: the strategic-sufficiency regime can approach that ceiling only when the skill-stock term is present in the initial conditions — conversion architecture runs on fungible pre-existing skills, and a polity that has already spent its skill stock cannot surge.

### 8.3 The Distributional Stress-Test and the V6 Regime Comparison

The distributional stress-test of DP-EP-6 is wired into the V6 regime comparison, where the three trade regimes — *globalist_efficiency* (dependency drifting +0.015/yr, masked by prosperity gains), *autarkic_dominance* (dependency suppressed at growth and innovation cost), and *strategic_sufficiency* (dependency converging to 0.25 with industrial capacity compounding) — each carry their education-finance modules: the efficiency regime's credential market produces the §1 dysfunctions (degree inflation, thin materiality pool, debt concentration); the autarkic regime produces §4.3's manpower-planning misallocation; and the sufficiency regime carries the DP-EP bundle. Student debt enters as the distress-exposure variable of §5.3: it raises the volatility of youth political-preference variables and interacts with underemployment to amplify anti-system voting, with elasticities parameterized from the trade-shock literature (Autor AER 2020; Fetzer AER 2019) rather than an assumed debt-to-radicalization law — and the simulation scores that distress's concentration by stratum as a social-fabric cost under the V5 family-integrity and values-transmission metrics. The result is a regime comparison in which education finance is a stability variable rather than a budget line: the polity can observe, in runs, that the aggregate-ROI test conceals distributional failure whose costs arrive later, through the volatility channel, at compound interest — precisely the observation a civilization-scale architecture exists to make visible before the default cliff makes it undeniable.
